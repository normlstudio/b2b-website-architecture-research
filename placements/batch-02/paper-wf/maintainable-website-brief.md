A useful website brief does more than describe the desired look. It records the business, content, technical, and operating decisions that later teams should not have to rediscover.

## State the job and the boundaries

I would begin with the audiences, buying questions, offers, evidence, primary actions, and measurable problems the website needs to address. The brief should also state exclusions: unsupported audiences, claims that require approval, integrations outside the release, and outcomes nobody can promise.

Those boundaries protect the work when new ideas arrive. A page or feature can be evaluated against the agreed job instead of winning because it sounds useful in isolation.

## Connect content and architecture

List the content types, owners, required fields, relationships, publishing states, and reuse rules. Then map the page families and navigation that present that content. This gives design and development a shared model rather than two parallel interpretations of the sitemap.

The brief should identify where proof lives and how it is maintained. Case studies, team records, service facts, statistics, and testimonials each need a source and review path.

## Define release and ownership

Record accessibility expectations, responsive behavior, SEO controls, analytics, consent, performance checks, browser support, QA evidence, deployment, rollback, documentation, and the team responsible after launch.
