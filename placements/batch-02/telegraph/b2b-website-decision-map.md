A B2B website is often treated as a set of pages to design. The harder job is deciding what each buyer needs to understand, what evidence supports the claims, and how the content will remain accurate after launch.

## Begin with decisions, not page names

I would map the decisions the site must support before drawing the navigation. A buyer may need to recognize a problem, compare an approach, understand fit, review relevant work, identify risk, and choose a next step. Different audiences may share part of that route while needing distinct evidence later.

That map helps the team decide which page owns each answer. It prevents the homepage, service pages, case studies, and resource library from repeating the same general promise without adding useful detail.

## Give every claim an evidence type

A service claim can be supported by a method, work example, measured outcome, qualification, policy, or named point of view. The right evidence depends on the claim. A client logo cannot explain how a technical decision was made, and a process diagram cannot prove a result it did not measure.

The content model should preserve those distinctions. Authors need fields and editorial rules that make the proof visible, current, and reusable without turning every page into the same template.

## Design the operating model with the interface

Navigation, content ownership, analytics, forms, integrations, QA, and release rules all affect what the buyer experiences. A website plan is incomplete if the team cannot say who updates a claim, tests an action, or repairs a broken handoff after launch.
